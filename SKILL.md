# AI writing tropes to avoid

Source: [tropes.fyi](https://tropes.fyi) by [ossama.is](https://ossama.is).

Any one of these used once can be fine. The tell is several together, or one repeated. Write varied, imperfect, specific prose.

## Word choice

### Magic adverbs
"Quietly", "deeply", "fundamentally", "remarkably", "arguably" added to make a mundane description feel significant.
- "quietly orchestrating workflows, decisions, and interactions"
- "a quiet intelligence behind it"

### "Delve" and friends
The overused AI vocabulary: "delve", "certainly", "utilize", "leverage" (as a verb), "robust", "streamline", "harness".
- "We certainly need to leverage these robust frameworks..."

### "Tapestry" and "landscape"
Ornate nouns where a plain word works. Also "paradigm", "synergy", "ecosystem", "framework".
- "Navigating the complex landscape of modern AI..."

### The "serves as" dodge
"Serves as", "stands as", "marks", "represents" used to avoid a plain "is".
- "The station marks a pivotal moment in the evolution of regional transit."

### Synonym cycling
Refusing to repeat a noun, so one thing gets several names in the same paragraph. Pick one word and keep it.
- "the dashboard... the interface... the portal... the analytics hub"

### "Where it actually lives"
Describing where something comes from as if it physically lives there, in place of a direct answer.
- "where the complexity actually lives"

## Sentence structure

### Negative parallelism
"It's not X, it's Y": the most commonly identified AI tell. It frames every point as a surprising reframe. Includes "not because X, but because Y", "X, not Y", and the cross-sentence version "The question isn't X. The question is Y."
- "It's not bold. It's backwards."
- "The real issue isn't technical. The real issue is organizational."

### "Not X. Not Y. Just Z."
Negating two or more things before the reveal, to fake a narrowing-down.
- "Not a bug. Not a feature. A fundamental design flaw."

### "The X? A Y."
A question nobody asked, answered immediately for drama.
- "The result? Devastating."
- "The worst part? Nobody saw it coming."

### Anaphora abuse
The same sentence opening repeated in quick succession.
- "They assume that users will pay... They assume that developers will build... They assume that..."

### Rule of three
Tricolons back to back, or stretched to four or five items. One is fine; a string of them is a pattern.
- "Products impress people; platforms empower them. Products solve problems; platforms create worlds."

### "It's worth noting"
Filler transitions that connect nothing: "it bears mentioning", "importantly", "interestingly", "notably".

### Superficial analyses
A trailing "-ing" phrase that attaches fake significance to a plain fact.
- "...contributing to the region's rich cultural heritage"
- "...underscoring its role as a dynamic hub of activity and culture"

### False ranges
"From X to Y" where X and Y sit on no real scale, so there is nothing in between.
- "From innovation to implementation to cultural transformation."

### Comma-clipped trailing phrase
A short tail hung off a comma so the sentence finishes sideways, sometimes a bare noun tacked on at the end.
- "asked forty times, mentoring."

## Paragraph structure

### Short punchy fragments
Very short sentences or fragments as standalone paragraphs for manufactured emphasis. Nobody writes first drafts this way.
- "He published this. Openly. In a book. As a priest."

### Excessive enumeration
A listicle disguised as prose: "The first... The second... The third...", often after being told not to use lists.
- "The first wall is the absence of a free, scoped API... The second wall is..."

## Tone

### "Here's the kicker"
False-suspense transitions before an ordinary point: "here's the thing", "here's where it gets interesting", "here's what most people miss", "here's the deal".

### "Think of it as..."
The patronizing analogy, "Think of it as..." or "It's like a...", often less clear than the original concept.
- "Think of it as a Swiss Army knife for your workflow."

### Forced figurative language
A simile or coined metaphor used because it sounds clever, not because it clarifies. Includes borrowing a word from the prompt as a metaphor for something unrelated.
- "like tracking your weight loss with a scale that you also control the calibration on"

### "Imagine a world where..."
Opening with a list of wonderful futures to sell the premise.

### False vulnerability
Polished, risk-free self-awareness that performs honesty. Real vulnerability is specific and uncomfortable.
- "And yes, I'm openly in love with the platform model."
- "This is not a rant; it's a diagnosis."

### "The truth is simple"
Asserting that a point is obvious instead of proving it, or "the real story is..." as a privileged reveal.
- "History is clear, the metrics are clear, the examples are clear."

### Grandiose stakes inflation
Every topic becomes world-historical.
- "This will fundamentally reshape how we think about everything."

### "Let's break this down"
Teacher voice aimed at readers who don't need it: "let's unpack this", "let's explore", "let's dive in".

### Vague attributions
"Experts argue", "industry reports suggest", "observers have cited", or one source inflated into "several publications". If you can't name the source, you don't have one.

### Appeal to familiarity
Claiming a thing is well known in place of evidence: "a classic", "famously", "notoriously", "as we all know".

### Invented concept labels
A domain word plus an abstract noun (paradox, trap, creep, divide, vacuum, inversion) used as if it were an established term.
- "the supervision paradox", "the acceleration trap", "workload creep"

### Compulsive counting
Stating the number of items before listing them, as if the count were the achievement.
- "Four reasons why this will work"

### Quotable one-liners
A line built to be pulled out as wisdom that carries no information.
- "When everything gets maximum emphasis, nothing has any."

### Promotional language
Writing like a brochure: selling the subject instead of describing it.
- "a seamless experience that elevates every part of your workflow"

### Collaborative "we"
Switching an author's "I" to "we", losing the personal voice. Some authors do use "we"; check before converting.
- "As we move forward, our focus shifts to execution."

## Formatting

### Em-dash addiction
Em dashes for every pause, aside and pivot. A person uses two or three in a piece; AI uses twenty.
- "The problem -- and this is the part nobody talks about -- is systemic."

### Bold-first bullets
Every list item opens with a bolded phrase. Common in AI-written docs and READMEs, rare in anything typed by hand.

### Unicode decoration
Arrows (→) and curly quotes that nobody types on a keyboard.

### "Where / What / Why" headers
Headings that start with a question word.
- "Where the market is stuck today", "What we do differently"

### Title case headings
Capitalizing every word of a heading instead of the first word and proper nouns.

## Composition

### Preamble
Announcing what's coming, restating the prompt, or clearing the throat instead of making the point.
- "Two constraints shape the design."
- "The more important point is where the decision itself sits."

### Reasoning leak
Narrating what the text or the model is doing or deciding instead of doing it.
- "I want to be exact about my own role here."

### Premise stacking
A paragraph of evidence placed before the point (often a question), so the point has been made two or three times before it arrives.

### Belaboring the unnecessary
Defending a minor point against an objection nobody was going to raise.
- "I don't mean any of that as cynicism about players."

### Fractal summaries
Every section previews and recaps itself, and so does the document.
- "In this section, we'll explore... as we've seen in this section."

### The dead metaphor
One metaphor repeated five to ten times across the piece instead of used once.

### Self-echo
Reusing an earlier word or phrase as if paying it off, when it's the same narrow vocabulary resurfacing.

### Historical analogy stacking
Rapid-fire companies or tech eras used to borrow authority.
- "Apple didn't build Uber. Facebook didn't build Spotify. Stripe didn't build Shopify."
- "Every major shift -- the web, mobile, social, cloud -- followed the same pattern."

### One-point dilution
One argument restated in many framings to seem comprehensive. An 800-word point becomes 4,000 words.

### Content duplication
The same section or paragraph appearing twice.

### Signposted conclusion
"In conclusion", "to sum up", "in summary". The reader can tell it's ending.

### The tie-back
Restating the answer and looping it back to the question after the answer is already given.
- "So, to answer your question: yes, the employee can be added to the app."

### Never-ending conclusion
An ending that stacks clause after clause because it won't stop.

### "Despite its challenges..."
Acknowledging a problem only to dismiss it with optimism.
- "Despite these challenges, the initiative continues to thrive."
